### No Priors — How Nuclear Will Unlock Energy Abundance with Valar Atomics Founder Isaiah Taylor

**The Takeaway:** Nuclear energy's biggest problem isn't physics or safety — it's that the industry has been a "modeling and simulation" industry for 40 years, and the only way to fix it is to actually build reactors, turn them on, and iterate like a hardware startup.

Isaiah Taylor, founder and CEO of Valar Atomics, is not your typical nuclear founder. He doesn't have decades of nuclear research credentials. What he has is a conviction that nuclear fission never had its "Ford moment" — and he's building it. Valar just became the first startup to ever make power from an advanced reactor, and they did it in under three years from founding.

Taylor's core insight is simple and devastating: most of the nuclear industry is a paper industry. Companies produce beautiful designs and simulations but never actually build anything. Valar does the opposite. They poured their own concrete (inventing a new grade that doesn't require rebar and won't become nuclear waste), built their own reactor protection system for $400,000 instead of paying a vendor $5 million and waiting 2.5 years, and turned on their first reactor under a Department of Energy pathway that had been sitting in legislation for 40 years.

The reactor itself is a TRISO-fueled, graphite-moderated, helium-cooled design that's passively safe — meaning if you turn off all power and all safety systems, the physics of the design itself prevents a meltdown. "We start with the worst case possible scenario where everything has failed, and we say, how do we make that safe?" Taylor explains. "And that's really a physics problem."

Valar's metric for success is "tick rate" — the time between splitting atoms in new reactors. Their first atom split took 2 years and 4 months from founding. The second took 7 months. Their goal is to get it down to minutes. "This company will get to the point where we have a new reactor turning on every few minutes," Taylor says.

When asked about AI's role in all this, Taylor is refreshingly honest: "Energy being a commodity, the demand is set by the price. If you can figure out how to make energy cheaper, you will have demand." He's grateful for the AI tailwinds but sees the market as fundamentally infinite — if you can make energy at 1 cent, you induce your own demand.

The most striking moment: Valar powered the first ever AI chip (an NVIDIA Blackwell) directly from their nuclear reactor and hosted a website on it. Go to nuclearwebsite.com while the reactor is running, and every page load tells you exactly how many atoms of uranium were split to deliver that web page.

Taylor's vision extends beyond just powering data centers. He sees a future where energy becomes the fundamental input for all manufacturing — where AI and robotics convert the human element of production into energy consumption, and where cheap energy makes "basically everything free." It's a bold, almost sci-fi vision, but standing in front of a reactor that's actually splitting atoms, it's hard to dismiss.
