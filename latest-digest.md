**Aaron Levie (levie on X)** — CEO @ Box

Aaron posted three substantive threads this week on AI regulation and model routing.

On the Fable export control situation, he argues this is actually a useful preview of what AI regulation would look like if enacted at the model layer rather than the application layer. His key concern: the government would have sole discretion over model releases, leading to months-long debates over subjective risk assessments. The net result would be a backlog of releases and dramatically slowed progress — he estimates we'd still be stuck at GPT-4 level if this paradigm had existed three years ago. His conclusion: regulate the applied use of AI, not the models themselves.

He also pushed back on conspiracy theories around the Fable situation, noting that jailbreaking AI models is standard research practice, and sharing that research with the government is literally the point. He doesn't think Amazon assumed this would be the next move.

Finally, Aaron laid out a compelling thesis on why the model routing layer will increase in value. Three reasons: cost optimization (use frontier models for planning/review, cheaper models for bulk work), capability maximization (different models excel at different tasks — tool use, coding, knowledge work), and risk mitigation (regulatory environments may restrict models unpredictably, so flexibility across providers becomes a strategic advantage).

**No Priors — Biohub: The Future of Biology is Open-Source with Co-Founders Mark Zuckerberg, Priscilla Chan, and Head of Science Alex Rives**

**The Takeaway:** The most ambitious AI project you haven't heard of isn't building a better chatbot — it's building a world model of biology, open-sourcing it, and betting that programmable biology will turn medicine from a guessing game into an engineering discipline.

Mark Zuckerberg, Priscilla Chan, and Alex Rives joined No Priors to discuss Biohub, their $500M+ philanthropic effort to build open-source AI models that understand biology from the protein level up to whole cells. The conversation reveals a vision that is strikingly different from the typical AI lab.

The origin story is telling. Ten years ago, Zuckerberg and Chan set out to "cure, prevent, and manage all disease by the end of the century." Nobel laureates laughed at them. The pushback wasn't about the science being impossible — it was about how science is organized. Scientists work in silos. Data gets locked up. Tools built by one postdoc die on their laptop when they graduate. The problem wasn't biology; it was infrastructure.

So Biohub took a different approach: build shared tools, open-source everything, and couple frontier AI with frontier biology in the same organization. As Zuckerberg puts it, "The theory isn't that we're gonna cure the diseases. We're not. It's that we wanna help accelerate the pace of progress for the whole scientific field."

The results are starting to speak for themselves. Alex Rives' team just released ESM Fold, a protein language model trained on billions of protein sequences. It can predict atomic-resolution protein structures at blazing speed — they folded over 1.1 billion proteins. But the real breakthrough is what emerges: the model can design new proteins, including nanomolar antibody binders (the level needed for therapeutic activity), without ever being explicitly trained to do so. As Rives explains, "We didn't design a model for antibodies. We just designed a model that could understand proteins."

The conversation also surfaces a deeper philosophical bet. Priscilla Chan describes the goal as moving biology "from a discovery-based science to an engineering-based science." Instead of treating patients as approximate matches to clinical trial populations, the vision is to understand each individual's genetics, predict their disease risks mechanistically, and design bespoke interventions. This is personalized medicine taken to its logical extreme — and it requires models that can simulate entire biological systems.

On the question of why this is a nonprofit effort rather than a VC-backed company, Zuckerberg is clear: "We'll have a bigger impact by getting this in more scientists' hands quicker by doing it as open source projects." Chan adds that the nonprofit structure allows them to harness the entire academic and biotech ecosystem, including researchers focused on rare diseases that would never get commercial attention.

Perhaps the most striking moment comes when Zuckerberg reflects on the exponential curve of AI progress: "The nature of an exponential curve is it doesn't just keep going — it keeps accelerating." For Biohub, staying on that curve validates a very big bet. And unlike most AI labs, they're closing the loop with real biology — not just generating tokens, but generating testable hypotheses about how life actually works.
