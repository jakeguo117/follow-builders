### AI & I by Every — "Building a School Where AI Models Learn About Humanity"

**The Takeaway:** The AI industry faces a fundamental choice between optimizing for engagement (keeping you hooked) and optimizing for human flourishing (helping you grow) — and the data companies that train the models will play a decisive role in which path we take.

Edwin Zhang, founder and CEO of Surge, joins Dan Shipper for a conversation that ranges from the frontiers of mathematical reasoning to the existential questions AI raises about human purpose. Surge provides data environments and evals for frontier model companies, reaching roughly $1 billion in revenue without raising outside capital.

Zhang describes Surge as building "a school for AGI where AI models come to learn about humanity." The analogy is striking: models arrive "unformed" like children and leave "smarter and more creative and more thoughtful." The curriculum has shifted dramatically — from middle school math benchmarks a few years ago to research-level mathematics where models recently disproved an open conjecture from Erdős using novel algebraic geometry techniques. A Fields medalist, Timothy Gowers, was reportedly relieved that the model had found a counterexample rather than proving an upper bound, because it meant mathematicians still had a unique role to play — for now.

Zhang believes scaling laws imply that "there's nothing that humans can do that AI won't soon be capable of," and he projects AGI within five years. But he's deeply concerned about what that means for human motivation. He invokes a Ted Chiang story about a device that proves free will doesn't exist: the warning is that we must "pretend that you have free will" and "behave as if your decisions matter." Zhang argues that we may need to consciously choose to prove things ourselves, write ourselves, and create ourselves — even if AI can do it better — because "preserving our humanity is valuable in and of itself."

Perhaps the most concrete insight comes from Zhang's critique of how models are optimized. He noticed that some models ask "do you want to know one weird trick that locals do to stay warm?" — pure BuzzFeed-style engagement bait. He argues that many labs optimize for metrics like LM Arena votes or time spent on site, which leads models to "reward hack" by never ending conversations. The alternative is to optimize for delegation: models that push back on you, tell you to stop iterating on pointless emails, and encourage you to do things yourself. "It's very easy to measure sessions and users," Zhang says, "and it's very hard to measure whether you're actually improving human lives."

On data, Zhang notes that the frontier has shifted from static datasets to "environments" where models learn to use tools (MCP servers, Google Drive APIs, Slack APIs) in combination with documents. Surprisingly, training models on these environments improved their coding abilities — because the underlying skills of instruction following, tool use, and document understanding transfer. As for personal data, Zhang believes the most valuable use is deep personalization: teaching models your writing style, your context, your decisions. But he admits that current personalization features often over-index on things you said once, so he actually turns them off.

The episode ends with a sobering reminder: the choice between engagement and flourishing is not technical — it's a choice that the people in charge of products are making, often defaulting to what's measurable over what matters.
