**Training Data — Simulating Humans at Scale: Simile's Joon Sung Park**

**The Takeaway:** We're approaching a world where you can simulate entire human societies — not just survey responses — and the most ambitious use case isn't corporate market research, but understanding the fundamental mechanics of civilization itself.

Joon Sung Park started as a Stanford researcher building "generative agents" in a simulated town called Smallville. Twenty-five AI agents with memory, planning, and reflection woke up, went to work, formed relationships, and — most surprisingly — spontaneously organized a Valentine's Day party. One agent even forgot the invitation. This wasn't programmed behavior; it emerged.

Park's thesis is simple: large language models have already encoded vast amounts of human behavior from training data. The question is how to extract it. His company, Simile, now partners with Fortune 500s like CVS to simulate customer populations. The process: interview real people (often asking them to "tell me the story of your life"), use that data to create digital agents, then let customers run thousands of experiments in simulation — concept testing, pricing studies, even earnings call rehearsals.

But Park's real ambition is bigger. "I do see an opportunity today to really take the first crack at building the simulation," he says. "I would not have said that even five years ago."

The counterintuitive insight: frontier models optimized for rationality are actually *worse* at simulating humans. "People are irrational. We have a lot of subjective values, preferences and tastes. So you actually start to see divergence in model size going up and its ability to predict and simulate human behavior." Simile is building what Park calls "the GPU of intelligence" — not a superhuman model, but one that's as human as possible, capturing diversity of values rather than optimizing for correctness.

The hardest problem: validation. Simile's simulations predict human behavior at 85% accuracy (measured by distribution similarity). But multi-agent simulations introduce compounding errors. Park distinguishes between "convergent" simulations (where small errors don't matter because outcomes trend toward a stable equilibrium) and "divergent" ones (like elections, where small perturbations cascade). The solution: run simulations hundreds of times and report confidence intervals, like bootstrap resampling.

Park's North Star? "Simulation can be the Hubble Telescope for human society." He envisions $100 million simulations running for months that answer fundamental questions about collective action, climate change, or the origins of democracy. "There's a Nobel Prize to be won there."
