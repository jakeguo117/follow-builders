### Training Data — Suno's Mikey Shulman: Everyone Can Make Music Now

**The Takeaway:** The act of creation itself is the entertainment — not the output. Suno's CEO argues that AI's most profound consumer use case isn't automating existing workflows, but unlocking a fundamentally new category: creative entertainment, where making is the fun part.

**Why you should care:** Suno is the fastest-growing consumer AI product most people haven't fully processed. At a $300M revenue run rate with 90% of daily users actively creating — not just consuming — it's quietly proving that AI can reshape how billions of people spend their leisure time. Founder Mikey Shulman, a physicist-turned-CEO, is one of the most thoughtful builders in the consumer AI space.

**The remix:**

Mikey Shulman didn't set out to build a music company. With a physics PhD from Harvard and a background in quantum computing, he and his co-founders originally thought generating music would be "a couple of orders of magnitude away" in compute requirements. But then they had breakthroughs in audio compression that changed everything.

The key insight? Suno's models don't know anything about music. No 12-tone scales, no instrument categories. "The more musical knowledge we give the model, the more constrained it will be," Shulman says. By modeling everything as raw sound — a continuous waveform sampled 48,000 times per second — the system can create things that never existed before. Trap with a sitar. Country with 808s. Microtonal music that no human composer would arrive at.

This technical choice has a profound philosophical consequence. Most people assume AI music means "AI-powered Spotify" — a more efficient way to consume. Shulman sees something else entirely. "Before Suno, basically everybody was a consumer of music," he says. "The crazy thing about Suno is that in any given day, 90% of the users are going to create something. And you're not creating it to go bring it elsewhere. People are creating music for the fun and enjoyment and fulfillment that comes with being creative."

He calls this "creative entertainment" — a category where the entertaining part is the act of making, not the finished product. The closest analogy is cooking: you can get a better meal at a restaurant, but people still cook because it's fun to make and consume what you made. Or Claude Code: "Most of the things I build are definitely not meant to be hosted in AWS and used by millions of people. I actually enjoy the act of building, and I enjoy the act of using the thing that I built."

On the "slop" debate: Shulman isn't bothered. "I made two songs with my five year old yesterday. Is that slop? 99.999% of the planet has no interest in hearing that. But that's really meaningful to me." He draws a parallel to bedroom producers in the early 2000s — 13-year-olds making beats on laptops. At the time, people worried about a flood of bad music. In hindsight, it was obviously a good thing. More music means more great music and entirely new genres.

The Warner Music partnership isn't about licensing — it's about building products that "could never have existed before." Interactive concerts where the audience co-creates with the artist. Song templates you can riff on and send back to a friend. Using your own voice so that "when I send you a song and you can hear me in it, that song will resonate much more than some nondescript voice."

Shulman's most contrarian bet? Making full songs instead of crisp audio. "Every single one of our competitors had way crisper audio, and everybody could hear one second of a Suno song and know, 'oh, that sounds like crap.'" But he bet that telling a story mattered more than perfect sound quality. It paid off.

The broader lesson: "I think it's just really undervalued to invest in the product and the UI and the UX to make sure that you're constantly delighting people. We don't think of ourselves as a technology company. We think of ourselves as a music company."

**Training Data — Suno's Mikey Shulman: Everyone Can Make Music Now**

**核心观点：** 创作行为本身就是娱乐，而不是最终的输出品。Suno CEO 认为，AI 最深刻的消费级应用不是自动化现有工作流程，而是解锁一个全新的品类——"创造性娱乐"（creative entertainment），其中"制作"本身就是乐趣所在。

**为什么你应该关注：** Suno 是大多数人还没有完全理解的、增长最快的消费级 AI 产品。它的年化收入已达到 3 亿美元，90% 的日活跃用户不是在消费，而是在主动创作——它正在悄悄地证明，AI 可以重塑数十亿人如何度过闲暇时间。创始人 Mikey Shulman 是一位物理学家出身的 CEO，也是消费级 AI 领域最有思想的建设者之一。

**深度摘要：**

Mikey Shulman 一开始并没有打算做音乐公司。这位拥有哈佛物理学博士学位、背景是量子计算的创始人，和联合创始人最初认为，生成音乐所需的算力"差了好几个数量级"。但后来他们在音频压缩方面取得了突破，一切由此改变。

关键洞察是什么？Suno 的模型对音乐一无所知。没有十二音阶，没有乐器分类。"我们给模型的音乐知识越多，它的约束就越大，"Shulman 说。通过将一切建模为原始声音——一个以每秒 48,000 次采样的连续波形——系统可以创造出从未存在过的东西。西塔琴配陷阱鼓。乡村音乐加 808 低音。微音音乐，是人类作曲家不会想到的。

这个技术选择带来了深刻的哲学后果。大多数人认为 AI 音乐意味着"AI 驱动的 Spotify"——一种更高效的消费方式。Shulman 看到的完全是另一回事。"在 Suno 出现之前，基本上每个人都是音乐的消费者，"他说。"疯狂的是，在 Suno 上，每天有 90% 的用户会去创作一些东西。而且你创作它，并不是为了把它带到别的地方去。人们创作音乐，是为了创作本身带来的乐趣、享受和成就感。"

他称之为"创造性娱乐"——在这个品类里，娱乐的部分是"制作"这个行为本身，而不是最终成品。最接近的类比是做饭：你在餐厅能吃到更好的饭菜，但人们仍然自己下厨，因为制作和享用自己做的东西本身就是一件有趣的事。或者像 Claude Code："我做的绝大多数东西肯定不会部署到 AWS 上给几百万人用。但我享受构建的过程，也享受使用自己构建的东西。"

关于"垃圾内容"（slop）的争论：Shulman 并不在意。"我昨天和我五岁的孩子一起做了两首歌。那是垃圾内容吗？全球 99.999% 的人对听那首歌毫无兴趣。但那对我来说非常有意义。"他将其类比为 2000 年代初期的卧室制作人——13 岁的孩子在笔记本电脑上做 beats。当时人们担心会涌入大量烂音乐。回过头看，这显然是一件好事。更多的音乐意味着更多的好音乐和全新的流派。

与华纳音乐的合作不是为了授权——而是为了构建"以前根本不可能存在"的产品。观众与艺术家共同创作的互动演唱会。你可以即兴发挥并回传给朋友的音乐模板。使用你自己的声音，这样"当我发给你一首歌，你可以在里面听到我的声音时，那首歌会比某个千篇一律的声音更能打动你"。

Shulman 最反常规的赌注是什么？制作完整的歌曲，而不是追求完美的音质。"我们的每一个竞争对手的音质都更清晰，每个人听一秒 Suno 的歌就知道，'哦，这听起来真烂。'"但他赌的是，讲述一个故事比完美的音质更重要。这个赌注赢了。

更广泛的启示："我认为，在产品和 UI/UX 上投入，持续给用户带来惊喜，这一点被严重低估了。我们不把自己看作一家科技公司。我们把自己看作一家音乐公司。"
