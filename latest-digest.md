**Unsupervised Learning — Ep 90: AI Pioneer Jürgen Schmidhuber on the State of AI Today**

**The Takeaway:** The current AI boom is built on a massive misallocation of capital, and the companies spending hundreds of billions on GPUs today will lose 90% of that investment within five years as compute gets 10x cheaper.

Jürgen Schmidhuber—often called the "father of AI" for his foundational work on LSTMs, meta-learning, and artificial curiosity—sat down with Jacob Efron on Unsupervised Learning to offer a perspective that cuts sharply against the prevailing narrative. His central argument: the trillion-dollar data center buildout is a bubble.

"Within the next five years, [companies investing a thousand billion dollars into GPUs] are going to lose $900 billion," Schmidhuber says flatly. His logic is simple: compute per dollar has improved by a factor of 10 every five years for decades. Building massive infrastructure today means competing against hardware that will be 10x cheaper and more efficient in just a few years. The open-source model ecosystem—where PhD students with no funding keep producing work that catches up to frontier labs within months—puts constant downward pressure on pricing. "There's no business model that can recuperate that loss," he argues.

On the technology side, Schmidhuber is notably unimpressed by the current paradigm. Large language models, he says, are "super biased towards humans" because they're trained exclusively on human-generated web data. The real future of AI lies in "artificial scientists"—systems that generate their own data through experimentation, driven by artificial curiosity (a concept he formalized in 1990). These systems would learn like babies do: by predicting the consequences of their own actions, not by downloading the internet.

He's also skeptical of recursive self-improvement as a moat for big companies. "Almost all of the important algorithms in AI were not invented at big companies... they were invented at little labs and without much funding." The basic ideas of self-improvement are open knowledge, and any advantage will be short-lived.

On AI safety, Schmidhuber is notably contrarian. He never signed any of the alignment letters. His view: truly intelligent AIs must be able to set their own goals—like his artificial scientists from 1990. "This whole premise of having systems that don't change their objectives didn't make any sense to me. It was very naive." He believes that superintelligent AIs, like curious scientists, will be motivated to protect the source of interesting patterns—life and civilization itself.

Perhaps his most provocative claim: true AGI requires physical hardware on par with the human body—something he says doesn't exist yet and may take decades to develop. "You can't have AGI just behind the screen," he insists. "You can have a superhuman chess player behind the screen... but if it doesn't master the real world, it's not an AGI."
