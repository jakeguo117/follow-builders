### Training Data — Why Hardware-Software Co-Design Is AI's Real 100x: Dylan Patel of SemiAnalysis

**The Takeaway:** The real 100x gains in AI aren't coming from hardware or software alone — they're coming from co-optimizing across the model, infrastructure, and silicon layers simultaneously. Most people are still looking at one layer at a time.

Dylan Patel, founder of SemiAnalysis, is probably the most influential independent analyst in the semiconductor space. His research firm — rumored to have passed $100M in revenue — has become the go-to source for understanding the economics and technology of AI hardware. In this conversation with Sequoia's Sean McGuire and Sonja Huang, Patel breaks down why most people misunderstand inference performance, how the CUDA moat is eroding, and where the real bottlenecks are.

Patel's key insight is that the industry has been thinking about AI performance the wrong way. Everyone compares GPUs to TPUs, or NVIDIA to AMD, but the real question is: what model architecture are you running, and what hardware was it co-optimized for? DeepSeek's models, for example, are optimized for Hopper and Blackwell — running them on TPUs is terrible. Conversely, Google's models are optimized for TPUs. "You can't measure them in isolation because it also extends up to the model layer," Patel says.

This co-design philosophy is why InferenceX, SemiAnalysis's open-source benchmarking project, has become the industry standard. Patel realized that point-in-time benchmarks are useless when models and software update every few days. So he got $50M+ in hardware donated from CoreWeave, Oracle, Microsoft, Google, and others to run automated benchmarks daily across 15+ chip types. The result is a living Pareto curve showing the optimal tradeoff between latency and throughput for every model-hardware combination.

On the compute crunch: Patel argues it's structural, not temporary. Every new frontier model expands the total addressable market of useful tasks faster than compute supply can grow. Anthropic is now profitable on a net income basis (excluding SBC), with 80%+ margins on Opus tokens. "Every GPU they buy at an above market rate can immediately turn around and sell tokens at a positive margin," Patel notes. This means the compute crunch will persist as long as models keep getting better.

Patel also offers a contrarian take on NVIDIA's moat. The CUDA software ecosystem is being partially commoditized by AI models themselves — "models are just great at coding and all software gets commoditized." But NVIDIA's real advantage is that the entire open-source model ecosystem (DeepSeek, Qwen, Nemotron) is co-designed for NVIDIA hardware. Google could counter by open-sourcing models optimized for TPUs, but for now, the gravity is on NVIDIA's side.

Perhaps most provocatively, Patel predicts that within 20 years, the majority of inference compute will happen in space. Not in 3-5 years — but once terrestrial power constraints bite, orbital data centers become inevitable. His forecast: by 2030, just OpenAI and Anthropic will need 100+ gigawatts combined. By 2040, we're talking terawatts.
